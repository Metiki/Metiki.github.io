Job market paper

Where the Windfall Lands: Export Booms and the Demand for Democracy in Africa

I find that demand for democracy rises more in districts where more residents farm when world prices of their export crops rise.

Paper

Abstract

Do economic windfalls strengthen popular demand for democracy? Modernization theory predicts that higher household incomes increase demand for democracy by reducing reliance on political patrons, while rentier theories predict that windfalls captured by the state reinforce patronage and dependence. I link 125,659 Afrobarometer respondents (2005–2022) in 2,198 districts of 22 African countries to baseline crop maps and world prices. In a shift-share design that controls for expected price exposure, I compare districts with higher and lower shares of residents employed in farming. I find that demand for democracy rises more in districts where more residents farm when world prices of their export crops rise. For a one-standard-deviation three-year boom, the response is 0.025 standard deviations higher in a district with a 25-percentage-point higher farming share (p = 0.061 under a randomization test over the six price series). This is consistent with windfalls supporting democracy where they reach households and equally with state patronage lowering demand where few residents farm.

About

I am a researcher at the Kiel Institute for the World Economy and a doctoral candidate in Quantitative Economics at Kiel University, where I submitted my dissertation in November 2026. My research is in development economics and political economy, and most of it is on African countries.

Most African countries lack the tax and administrative-register data used to record incomes in high-income countries, and household surveys that measure consumption are expensive and infrequent. Much of my work therefore starts with measurement. With Tobias Heidland, I map where Africa’s middle class lives: a machine-learning model predicts middle-class shares for almost 160,000 populated cells from survey incomes, infrastructure, night lights and population data. In a second paper, I impute household consumption into Afrobarometer, the principal pan-African attitude survey. The data of both papers are public on Zenodo.

My job market paper asks whether citizens’ demand for democracy changes when export gains are more likely to reach households. I link 125,659 Afrobarometer respondents in 22 countries to crop maps and world prices, and I use the imputed consumption to test whether gains in household living standards accompany the response. A separate paper with Tobias Heidland and Lukas Menkhoff measures how central banks prepare for foreign exchange intervention and has been revised and resubmitted to the Journal of Money, Credit and Banking.
