Research

My research is in development economics and political economy. I measure economic welfare in African countries where official statistics are sparse, and I study how economic gains relate to political attitudes. My job market paper relates export booms to the demand for democracy. Two papers estimate where Africa’s middle class lives and which survey respondents belong to it. A fourth paper, with Tobias Heidland and Lukas Menkhoff, studies how central banks prepare for foreign exchange intervention. My next projects ask how much of a rise in world crop prices reaches farming households and how much the state retains.

Job Market Paper

Where the Windfall Lands: Export Booms and the Demand for Democracy in Africa

Job market paper Last updated November 2026

I find that demand for democracy rises more in districts where more residents farm when world prices of their export crops rise.

Paper

Do economic windfalls strengthen popular demand for democracy? Modernization theory predicts that higher household incomes increase demand for democracy by reducing reliance on political patrons, while rentier theories predict that windfalls captured by the state reinforce patronage and dependence. I link 125,659 Afrobarometer respondents (2005–2022) in 2,198 districts of 22 African countries to baseline crop maps and world prices. In a shift-share design that controls for expected price exposure, I compare districts with higher and lower shares of residents employed in farming. I find that demand for democracy rises more in districts where more residents farm when world prices of their export crops rise. For a one-standard-deviation three-year boom, the response is 0.025 standard deviations higher in a district with a 25-percentage-point higher farming share (p = 0.061 under a randomization test over the six price series). This is consistent with windfalls supporting democracy where they reach households and equally with state patronage lowering demand where few residents farm.

Under Review

Preparing for FX Intervention

with Tobias Heidland and Lukas Menkhoff

Revised and resubmitted, Journal of Money, Credit and Banking Last updated November 2026

Between 2001 and 2020, preparedness rose with more rigid regimes and larger reserves, while aggregate intervention did not keep pace.

Paper

Presented at the EUROFRAME Conference, Kiel (2024).

Most central banks do not publish their foreign exchange interventions (FXI), but the preparations that enable their interventions are visible. We measure FXI preparedness through three policy decisions, namely the rigidity of the exchange rate regime, the level of official reserves relative to GDP and the share of reserves held in assets available for intervention. For the last of these, we build a new index from the IMF’s data on reserve composition. Better-prepared countries intervene more, so preparedness indicates a willingness to intervene where intervention data are not published. Between 2001 and 2020, preparedness rose with more rigid regimes and larger reserves, while aggregate intervention did not keep pace. Preparedness is higher in economies with lower income, greater trade openness and more volatile portfolio inflows.

Working Papers

Estimating Economic Class in Afrobarometer Using Survey-to-Survey Imputation

Working paper Last updated November 2026

I impute consumption into Afrobarometer using a model estimated on 49 household-expenditure surveys in 25 countries, with 100 draws per respondent calibrated to the World Bank’s national distributions.

Paper · Data · Variable mapping and code

Afrobarometer, the principal pan-African attitude survey, omits consumption and income. As a result, the economic class of its respondents is unknown. I impute consumption into Afrobarometer using a model estimated on 49 household-expenditure surveys in 25 countries, with 100 draws per respondent calibrated to the World Bank’s national distributions. Each draw is classified under five published middle-class definitions, for adults in Rounds 8 and 9 (the adult series) and for households in Rounds 3 to 9 (the household series). In hold-out tests, the share at or above the Banerjee–Duflo upper bracket has a mean absolute error of 2.3 percentage points for an excluded survey wave and 7.0 for an excluded country. That share averages 28.5 percent across adult country-rounds, and bounded middle-class shares run from 9.8 to 68.5 percent across definitions. In the household series the middle-class share is higher by a median of 25.2 points in urban than in rural households and by 23.7 points for respondents with secondary education.

Mapping Africa’s Middle Class Across Space

with Tobias Heidland

Working paper Last updated November 2026

We find that 20.2 percent of Africans fall in the $2–10 band and 2.6 percent in the $10–100 band. Two-thirds of both groups live within 300 km of the coast.

Paper · Data

Presented at the MIASA Policy Conference, Accra (2023), a workshop of the Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs, Berlin (2024), and the German Development Economics Conference, Frankfurt (2025).

We provide the first highly granular mapping of the African middle class. Using incomes from the Gallup World Poll to calculate subnational middle-class shares in 45 countries between 2015 and 2023, infrastructure, night lights, population, and gridded measures of output and wealth, we then use a machine learning approach to predict shares for 159,698 populated cells. In leave-one-country-out cross-validation, the model explains 72 percent of the regional variation in the share of people living on $2–10 a day and 62 percent for $10–100 a day. Our approach cannot predict relative definitions well. We find that 20.2 percent of Africans fall in the $2–10 band and 2.6 percent in the $10–100 band. Two-thirds of both groups live within 300 km of the coast. Both are concentrated in regions where aid disbursements are small relative to GDP.

Next Projects

Farm-Gate Prices and the Politics of Export Booms

The project asks whether demand for democracy responds more strongly where a larger share of a price increase reaches producers.

Who Receives the Boom

The project asks how much of a world-price rise reaches farm sales and household consumption. It compares 431 households in Uganda’s national panel survey that report coffee sales in both the 2018/19 and the 2019/20 waves.

The State’s Side of the Boom

The project asks whether booms shift public spending and public employment toward capitals and whether urban attitudes follow. It uses Afrobarometer and Mali’s budget execution data by region and year.
